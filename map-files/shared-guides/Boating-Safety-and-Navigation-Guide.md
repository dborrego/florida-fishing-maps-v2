# Boating Safety and Navigation Guide

FloridaFishingMaps.com

## Start With The Right Mindset

GPS fishing coordinates are a tool for finding productive water. They are not a substitute for seamanship, local knowledge, current charts, or a safe route plan. Every reef, wreck, ledge, bridge, channel edge, and offshore waypoint should be approached with normal navigation discipline: slow down before arrival, watch depth trends, keep a lookout, and confirm what your electronics are showing against real water conditions.

South Florida changes fast. Wind against current can stack short, steep seas along the reef line. Afternoon thunderstorms can build from harmless clouds to dangerous lightning in minutes. The Gulf Stream can make a calm forecast feel completely different once you clear the inlet. Treat every trip as a fresh decision, even if you have fished the same spot many times.

## Before You Leave The Dock

Check the marine forecast, radar, tides, and inlet conditions before launching. For Miami and the Keys, pay close attention to wind direction and speed. East and southeast winds can make the reef edge sporty, especially when the Gulf Stream is tight to shore. North wind after a front can make the Stream rough and confused. Summer west winds may look friendly near the dock but can still bring strong afternoon storms.

Build a float plan. Tell someone where you are launching, the general area you plan to fish, who is on board, and when you expect to return. Charge phones, but do not rely on phones alone offshore. Cell service drops quickly in parts of the Keys backcountry and offshore Miami.

Bring more water than you think you need. Heat stress catches fishermen off guard, especially while bottom fishing or running long distances between spots. Sunscreen, hats, sunglasses, and shade matter as much as bait and tackle on long summer days.

## Electronics Checklist

Before the trip, power on your chartplotter and verify that the imported waypoints appear where expected. Zoom in and confirm several spots in the region you plan to fish. If your unit supports it, back up your existing waypoints before importing new data. Many units merge new files cleanly, but a backup protects your personal numbers.

Make sure your chart card is current. Fishing spots may sit near reefs, channels, wrecks, or shallow areas. The coordinate gets you near the structure; your chart, sonar, eyes, and judgment keep you safe getting there.

Carry a second reference when possible: another GPS, a phone or tablet with offline charts, a handheld GPS, or printed coordinate sheets. Electronics fail. SD cards corrupt. Batteries die. Redundancy is cheap compared to a lost day or an unsafe return.

## Approaching A Spot

Never run full speed directly to a waypoint in unfamiliar water. Approach from a safe direction using your chart, depth sounder, and visual cues. In the Keys, especially near patch reefs and backcountry edges, depth can change quickly. In Miami, wreck and reef spots may sit near other boats, dive flags, trap lines, or current breaks.

When you get within a quarter mile, slow down and start reading the water. Watch your sounder for the structure rather than assuming the coordinate is the exact best anchor point. Many productive areas are zones, not a single dot. Current, wind, and boat traffic determine where to set up.

If anchoring, calculate your swing. Do not anchor directly on reef structure. For reef and wreck fishing, set up up-current so baits drift naturally to the target while the boat stays clear of hazards. On artificial reefs and wrecks, watch for other boats already positioned. Give them room.

## Weather Awareness

South Florida weather rewards early starts. Summer mornings can be calm and excellent, while afternoons bring heat, squalls, and lightning. If storms begin building over the Everglades, Biscayne Bay, or the Keys island chain, keep an escape route in mind.

Lightning is non-negotiable. If thunder is close enough to hear clearly, you are already in range. Tall antennas, outriggers, T-tops, and wet decks make small boats vulnerable. Do not wait for rain before reacting to lightning.

Cold fronts bring different risks. In winter, a front can shift wind direction quickly and turn an easy run home into a wet, slow ride. Watch for steep waves at inlets, especially where outgoing tide meets incoming swell.

## VHF Radio Basics

A fixed-mount VHF radio is one of the best safety tools on a boat. Keep it on and monitor Channel 16. Use Channel 16 for distress and hailing, then move to a working channel when appropriate. Know how to give your position clearly using latitude and longitude from your GPS.

If your radio has DSC distress capability, register and program the MMSI number before relying on it. DSC is only useful when configured correctly and connected to GPS.

Useful habits:
- Do a radio check before long offshore runs.
- Keep handheld VHF backup charged.
- Speak slowly and clearly.
- Know your boat name, location, number of people onboard, and nature of emergency.

## Required And Smart Safety Gear

Carry properly sized life jackets for every person onboard and keep them accessible. Children and weak swimmers should wear them, not just have them stored. Bring a throwable flotation device, fire extinguisher, sound-producing device, visual distress signals, anchor with enough line, first-aid kit, bilge pump, navigation lights, and a working kill switch.

For offshore Miami, the Keys reef line, and long runs to humps or deep water, add extra layers of safety: EPIRB or PLB, handheld VHF, spare battery pack, basic tools, spare fuses, extra water, and a ditch bag. The farther you run, the less forgiving small problems become.
